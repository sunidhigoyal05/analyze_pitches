Rhetoric hidden inside a judging rubric

The Pitch Score

Five criteria grade a startup in twelve minutes — Problem & Fit, Tech, Evidence of Impact, Scale & Sustainability, Team. They read like a scoring checklist. They're also a script for the three oldest tools of persuasion: Pathos, Logos, and Ethos. Here's where each one hides, and why moving it out of place is what makes a technically correct pitch fall flat.

In plain English

  1. Open with a story, not a slide — but only for the first couple of minutes.
  2. For almost everything in the middle, judges want proof, not feeling — numbers, mechanisms, evidence they could check themselves.
  3. Save "why you can be trusted" for the end — that's when it does the most work, once everything else has been said.
Pathos — the Heart

The stakes — who the problem hurts, and why a stranger in the room should care.

Logos — the Head

The logic — mechanism, market math, evidence a judge could go verify.

Ethos — the Trust

The credibility — why this team, with this evidence, can be trusted to execute.

0:00–2:30 · Problem & Product-Market Fit

Earn the emotional hook before you earn the market slide

Pathos-led + Logos

Judges score this as "is there a real problem, and is there evidence someone already wants it solved." Rhetorically it's the one stretch where leading with Pathos is correct: a specific person, a specific moment, a number that makes the scale of the pain undeniable. Wait past the two-minute mark to introduce market-sizing logic and the hook is gone before it had a chance to matter.

2:30–5:30 · Tech

Emotional framing here reads as compensating

Logos-led

This is the rubric's most Logos-heavy stretch — mechanism, defensibility, why the approach works and why it's hard to copy. Pathos mostly disappears here on purpose: a judge scoring "tech" is checking whether the how holds up, and a sudden emotional beat in the middle of a technical explanation reads as a shaky answer in disguise.

5:30–7:30 · Evidence of Impact

Data and story have to share the stage

Logos + Pathos

"Evidence of impact" is really asking for two things a judge weighs together: a dataset proving the intervention works, and a story of one person or community it worked for. Data alone reads like an academic abstract. Story alone reads like an anecdote. This is the one rubric line built to reward holding both at once.

7:30–9:30 · Scale & Sustainability

The plan for scale is itself a credibility signal

Logos + rising Ethos

By now a judge already believes the problem is real and the solution works; what's being scored is whether the model survives contact with the real world — unit economics, distribution, what breaks at 10x. Logos still leads, but Ethos starts entering: having a credible plan for scale signals credibility on its own, separate from whatever the plan's numbers say.

9:30–12:00 · Team Composition

Everything said before is now on trial

Ethos-led

Team is the rubric's Ethos slot, almost undiluted — why these specific people, with this specific history, are the ones who execute. It closes the pitch because credibility is cumulative: everything said in the previous nine minutes is what a judge is now deciding whether to believe, and "who is telling me this" is the last variable left to weigh.

This page is the model, not the measurement — a hypothesis for where each appeal should sit. It's since been tested against real judged pitches, MLK, Steve Jobs, and Airbnb's original seed deck: see The Appeal Arc for where this arc held up, and the one place real pitches broke the pattern entirely (they open on data, not on feeling).
