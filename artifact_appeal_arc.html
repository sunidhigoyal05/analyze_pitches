The Appeal Arc

The rubric hypothesis guessed which appeal should dominate each slice of a pitch. This page codes six real judged pitches and four classic VC decks the same way, window by window or slide by slide, to see where Pathos, Logos, and Ethos actually show up — then closes with two legendary speeches, MLK and Steve Jobs, coded for the same three appeals as a vivid, non-pitch contrast case.

In plain English

We watched each speech or pitch in roughly 45-second chunks and asked, for each chunk: was this mostly an appeal to the Heart (emotion), the Head (facts and logic), or Trust (the speaker's own credibility)? Taller, more colorful bars below mean that appeal is landing harder in that moment. Hover anything for the exact words that earned the label — no hovering required to get the big picture, though: read the one-line takeaway under each chart.

Pathos — the Heart

The stakes — who it hurts, why a stranger should care.

Logos — the Head

The logic — mechanism, numbers, verifiable claims.

Ethos — the Trust

The credibility — why this speaker can be trusted.

Takeaway

These real pitches — winners and finalists alike — talk mostly in facts and numbers (blue), almost start to finish. Emotion and trust-building show up as brief, deliberate moments, not the main event, and that's true whether the pitch is about ed-tech, transit, or rare-disease diagnostics.

Where the rubric actually applies: four classic VC decks

These four decks were built to satisfy exactly this kind of reading — Problem & Fit, Tech, Evidence of Impact, Scale & Sustainability, Team — the way the pitches above were, but with no judges or clock in the room: Airbnb (2008 seed), Uber/UberCab (2008 pre-seed), Dropbox (2007 seed), and LinkedIn (2004 Series B). None has timing data (they're static slides, not recordings), so each column below is one slide, not one minute.

How to read each grid: the top row is which appeal (Heart/Head/Trust) leads each slide. The five rows under it are the rubric's five scoring criteria — a colored cell means that slide addresses that criterion; gray means it doesn't.

Takeaway

Across all four decks, "Evidence of Impact" barely registers — these are business pitches, not social-impact ones, so that row stays almost entirely gray. Two decks (Uber, Dropbox) skip a dedicated Team slide entirely; the two that do include one (Airbnb, LinkedIn) both place it at the very end, the same closing position as the real judged pitches above.

For contrast: two master orators, no rubric at all

Neither MLK nor Jobs was ever scored against Problem & Fit, Tech, Evidence of Impact, Scale & Sustainability, or Team — there was no rubric in the room for either. They're here as a closing contrast case: bar height is coded rhetorical intensity, not just which appeal is present, the closest thing to a seismograph for a speech. Dots above the chart mark verbatim highlight moments; hover any bar or dot for the transcript detail.

Takeaway

King builds trust first, then leans on emotion almost non-stop until the very end. Jobs never settles — every "wow" moment is immediately followed by a hard spec, back and forth, start to finish. Both are far spikier and more theatrical than anything in the real pitches or decks above — which is exactly the point of putting them last.
